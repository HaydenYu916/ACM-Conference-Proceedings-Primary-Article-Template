\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}
This section presents the three components that address the challenges
in \S\ref{sec:introduction}. Section~\ref{sec:design-frontend} defines a
jointly learned visual frontend that keeps edge computation small,
\S\ref{sec:design-objective} trains it against the frozen policy's own
behavior, and \S\ref{sec:design-temporal} codes successive uploads without
a neural decoder on the robot.

\subsection{Jointly Learned Visual Frontend}
\label{sec:design-frontend}
We replace the image path of a frozen VLA policy with a learned visual
frontend that maps camera images directly to the policy's visual tokens.
This subsection first defines the interface that the frontend must
reproduce, then describes the edge and cloud halves that realize it.

\paragraph{Recovery target.}
Let $x_t^{(c)}$ denote the image from camera $c$ at upload $t$. In the
original policy, a visual encoder $T$ and a projection $P$ map each image
to visual tokens
\begin{equation}
  z_t^{(c)} = P\big(T(x_t^{(c)})\big),
  \label{eq:teacher_tokens}
\end{equation}
which the policy consumes together with the language instruction and the
robot state to produce an action chunk. We refer to the boundary at which
$z_t^{(c)}$ enters the policy as the policy visual-input interface. The
frontend must produce recovered tokens $\hat{z}_t^{(c)}$ with the same
shape and arrangement as $z_t^{(c)}$, and every policy weight after this
interface remains frozen. In the primary $\pi_{0.5}$ configuration, $T$
is the SigLIP tower~\cite{siglip}, and each camera contributes 256 tokens
of dimension 2,048.

\paragraph{Edge analysis transform.}
Following the convention of learned compression, we treat all
robot-side computation as a single analysis transform $g_a$ followed by
scalar quantization $Q$:
\begin{equation}
  y_t^{(c)} = Q\big(g_a(x_t^{(c)})\big), \qquad g_a = h \circ s .
  \label{eq:analysis}
\end{equation}
The transform has two stages. The direct choice for its first stage
would be the original tower $T$ itself, but running $T$ on the robot at
every upload is too slow for an edge device (\S\ref{sec:bg-cost}).
We therefore use a student trunk $s$ with 12 transformer layers of width
768 and about 85 million parameters, compared with 415 million for the
teacher path $P \circ T$. A bottleneck head $h$, a small transformer,
maps the trunk features to a 256-dimensional latent per token, and $Q$
rounds each latent value to one of five levels with step size 4 and
bound 8. Every camera stream passes through $g_a$; no camera is sent as
an image.

\paragraph{Cloud synthesis transform.}
In the cloud, a synthesis transform $g_s$ with four transformer layers
of width 512 maps the recovered codes to policy tokens,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{z}_t^{(c)} = g_s\big(y_t^{(c)}\big),
  \label{eq:synthesis}
\end{equation}
which enter the policy in place of $P(T(\cdot))$. The cloud therefore
runs neither an image decoder nor the original visual tower, and no
image is reconstructed at any point in the deployed path. Because $g_s$
targets one policy's interface, it is trained per policy.

\paragraph{One trainable path.}
The student trunk is initialized by distillation from the teacher path
$P \circ T$, but it is not frozen afterwards. The trunk $s$, the head $h$, and the decoder $g_s$
are updated together in every end-to-end training stage, with gradients
passing through the frozen policy (\S\ref{sec:design-objective}). In
the final LIBERO model, the trunk accounts for 85 million of the 105.2
million trainable parameters. The trunk thus acts as the feature
extractor of the codec rather than as a fixed copy of the teacher, in the
same way that a learned image codec may initialize its analysis
transform from a pretrained network before training the whole model.

\paragraph{Across policies.}
The structure of the frontend does not depend on the policy. What changes
from one policy to another is the target interface, which sets the output
shape of $g_s$, and the form of the action term, which follows the
policy's action parameterization (\S\ref{sec:design-objective}). Each
policy therefore receives its own trained frontend while its weights stay
frozen. Section~\ref{sec:impl-backbones} gives the configurations for
$\pi_{0.5}$ and OpenVLA-OFT.

\subsection{Policy-Aligned Training}
\label{sec:design-objective}
We train the frontend so that the frozen policy, when fed recovered
tokens, behaves as it does on clean images, while the codes stay within
a byte budget. This subsection describes the training objective and the
staged schedule that introduces quantization and temporal coding.

\paragraph{Objective.}
During training, a clean teacher path computes the reference tokens
$z_t$ of Equation~\ref{eq:teacher_tokens} and the policy's behavior on
them. The frontend parameters ($s$, $h$, $g_s$) are updated, and the
policy weights stay frozen. The loss in Equation~\ref{eq:loss} combines three main terms with two
auxiliary terms:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tok}} + \alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}}
  + \rho(\tau)\big(\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rate}}
  + \beta\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{grp}}\big) + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}} .
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
The token term $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tok}}$ is the mean-squared error
between $\hat{z}_t$ and $z_t$, divided by the same error for tokens
computed from random inputs; it anchors the recovered tokens to the
policy's visual-input interface. The action term
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}}$ evaluates the frozen policy's
flow-matching loss under $\hat{z}_t$, with targets taken from the
policy's own actions on clean inputs, and normalizes it to the position
between the losses obtained with clean tokens and with random tokens.
The rate term $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rate}}$ is the payload per upload
estimated from the entropy model used by rANS. The two normalizations
place $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tok}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}}$ on
comparable scales, so their weights do not directly indicate their
relative contribution to the gradient.

\paragraph{Why an action term.}
The direct objective would be the token term alone, which asks the
recovered tokens to resemble the original ones and treats all deviations
uniformly. Resemblance, however, does not guarantee the same behavior:
some deviations leave the policy's output unchanged, whereas others alter
it, and inputs that look close to the original can still change the
policy's actions (\S\ref{sec:bg-align}). The action term weights reconstruction errors
by their effect on the frozen policy's output, so the limited byte
budget is spent on the visual information that the controller uses.
The action targets are outputs of the original policy rather than
additional expert annotations; training therefore requires no action
labels.

\paragraph{Auxiliary terms.}
The group term $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{grp}}$ applies an $\ell_2$ penalty
to groups of latent coefficients, encouraging whole groups to become zero
and therefore cheap to code. The prior term $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}$
fits the entropy model to the empirical distribution of the quantized
coefficients and updates only the entropy model. The ramp $\rho(\tau)$
increases the rate and group weights over training step $\tau$.
Section~\ref{sec:impl-backbones} lists the weights used for each policy.

\paragraph{Staged schedule.}
Training proceeds in three end-to-end stages, each initialized from the
previous one. The student trunk is first initialized by distillation from
the teacher path $P \circ T$. Stage S1 trains the whole frontend without
quantization. Stage F1 adds quantization and the rate terms for intra
uploads. Stage F2 adds temporal coding (\S\ref{sec:design-temporal}) and
rolls through intra and inter uploads in the order used at deployment.
The student trunk is updated in every stage. For OpenVLA-OFT, the action
term instead uses an L1 loss on the clean policy's action chunks, matching
that policy's action parameterization (\S\ref{sec:impl-backbones}).

\subsection{Decoder-Free Temporal Coding at the Edge}
\label{sec:design-temporal}
Consecutive uploads from the same camera overlap substantially, so each
code can be predicted from the previous one. We exploit this redundancy with
predictive coding in the space of quantized codes, and then convert the
resulting integer symbols into a bitstream with rANS.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\columnwidth]{images/temporal-reference.pdf}
  \caption{Predicting from the quantized code removes the neural decoder
  from the robot. (a) Predicting from a
  reconstructed reference requires a neural decoder on the robot at every
  upload. (b) Predicting from the previous quantized code $y_{t-1}$ only
  requires storing an integer copy, which the cloud also keeps. Orange
  boxes are neural modules; blue boxes are non-neural.}
  \Description{Two small top-to-bottom block diagrams of the robot side,
  side by side. In (a), a reference produced by a neural decoder is
  subtracted from the output of g_a before Q and rANS; the quantized output
  also feeds the decoder. In (b), g_a and Q produce a code; the previous
  code y_{t-1}, stored after Q, is subtracted before rANS. Neural modules
  are shaded orange and non-neural modules blue.}
  \label{fig:temporal-reference}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Prediction in the code domain.}
We organize uploads into groups of pictures (GOPs) of $G$ uploads. The
first upload of each GOP is an intra upload that transmits the code $y_t$
of Equation~\ref{eq:analysis} directly. Every later upload is an inter upload that transmits only the
residual against the code of the previous upload,
\begin{equation}
  r_t = y_t - y_{t-1}, \qquad y_t = y_{t-1} + r_t ,
  \label{eq:dpcm}
\end{equation}
where the second identity is evaluated by the receiver. Because $y_t$
and $y_{t-1}$ are integer codes, the residual is an integer and the
receiver recovers $y_t$ exactly. The primary $\pi_{0.5}$ configuration
uses $G=16$ and the OpenVLA-OFT configuration uses $G=8$. These
intervals count policy uploads, not camera frames or actuator steps.

\paragraph{Why the code is the reference.}
Video codecs predict each frame from a reconstructed reference, so the
encoder must also run the decoder
(Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-reference}a). In our setting, a reconstructed
reference would come from a neural decoder, which the robot would then
have to execute at every upload. Floating-point differences between robot
and cloud hardware could also make the two references diverge. Using the
quantized code $y_{t-1}$ as the reference removes both problems. The robot keeps its reference by storing the
code it has just sent, without any neural decoding, and both endpoints
hold bit-identical references, so no drift accumulates within a GOP
(Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-reference}b).
The receiver inverts Equation~\ref{eq:dpcm} first and only then applies
$g_s$ (Equation~\ref{eq:synthesis}).
Training stage F2 (\S\ref{sec:design-objective}) rolls through intra and
inter uploads in this order, so the frontend is trained under the same
reference states it encounters at deployment.

\paragraph{Integer bitstream.}
We entropy-code intra codes and inter residuals with static
rANS~\cite{ans}, using a 32-bit state and integer frequency tables
normalized to 15 bits. The coder performs no floating-point arithmetic,
so the same symbols and tables produce byte-identical streams on ARM
robot hardware and x86 servers. The rate term used in training is an
estimate from the entropy model; the $\pi_{0.5}$ payloads reported in
\S\ref{sec:evaluation} are measured from the actual rANS bytes.
